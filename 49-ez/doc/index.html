What is 49‑EZ?

49‑EZ is a phonetic code language designed to transliterate the Arabic text of the Quran into a simple, rule‑based Latin script using only the standard U.S. keyboard. Every Arabic letter, vowel, and diacritic is mapped to a corresponding character – numbers, letters, and symbols – preserving the authentic pronunciation, rhythm, and musicality of the original recitation.

The name 49‑EZ combines 49 (Furqan – The Criterion) and EZ (Easy / Ease). It is a bridge – a doorway – for anyone, anywhere, to hear the Quran as it was first uttered, even if they cannot read the Arabic script.

49 = Four9an = Furqan (الفرقان – The Criterion).
EZ = Easy / Ease – A bridge, a path, a doorway.

How to Read 49‑EZ – A Quick Start

49‑EZ is designed to be read by anyone, regardless of their native language. Here's how:

  • Letters like b, d, f, m, n, s, t, w, y, z – pronounce them exactly as you would in English.
  • Numbers like 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 0 – each represents a unique Arabic sound. The tables below show you how to pronounce each one.
  • Vowel marks like - (a), ° (u), _ (i) – they tell you which vowel sound to make. Short vowels are quick; long vowels (like --, °°, __) are held longer.
  • Special combos like d· (th as in "this") and t· (th as in "thin") – the middle dot tells you these two characters work together to make one sound.
  • Read it aloud, even if you don't understand the meaning. The sound itself is a form of connection – a way to hear the Quran as it was revealed.

Tip: Start with Surah Al-Fatihah (below) – it's short and contains many of the key sounds. Practice reading it aloud until it flows.

Some Arabic sounds are best represented by a combination of characters. These are the special combos used in 49‑EZ.

49‑EZ Arabic Name Pronunciation English Example Example (49‑EZ) Meaning

t· ث Thāʼ "th" as in "thin" (voiceless, sharp) Exactly like the "th" in "thin" or "thick" – sharp and unvoiced t·alath "three" (ثلاث)

English comparison: Think of it like the "th" in English – you don't pronounce the "t" and "h" separately; they make one sound together.

Tanween Details

The tanween (indefinite case endings) are represented by a combination of the vowel symbol and the = sign:

  • -= → "an" (fatḥatān) – like adding "an" to the end of a word
  • °= → "un" (ḍammatān) – like adding "un" to the end of a word
  • _= → "in" (kasratān) – like adding "in" to the end of a word

English comparison: Think of it like saying "a book" vs "the book" – the tanween adds a subtle "n" sound that indicates the word is indefinite.

Solar vs Lunar – A Living Example

Solar (L.)¨~r²-7m--n_ → "ar‑raḥmāni" (the L is assimilated, the sound flows)

This is the exact rule used in Arabic for sun and moon letters – preserved faithfully in 49‑EZ.

English comparison: Think of how the "n" in "an" sometimes changes before certain letters – like "a book" vs "an apple". In Arabic, the "L" in "al" disappears before "sun letters" (like "r" or "sh") and is pronounced fully before "moon letters" (like "b" or "m").

Pronunciation Guide for English Speakers

Here is a quick reference to help you produce the correct sounds when reading 49‑EZ.

Emphatic Letters

ṭ (4), ḍ (6), ṣ (0), ẓ (6·) – these are pronounced with the tongue tip pressed against the roof of the mouth, giving a "heavy" or "deep" sound.

English tip: Try saying "d" while pressing your tongue against the roof of your mouth – that's the feeling.

Guttural Sounds

kh (5) – like "ch" in "Bach".
gh (2) – like a French "r" but deeper.
ʻ (3) – a deep, throaty "a".
ḥ (7) – a strong, breathy "h".

English tip: These sounds come from the throat – practice by breathing deeply and using your throat muscles.

Th Sounds

d· – like "th" in "this" (voiced).
t· – like "th" in "thin" (voiceless).
6· – like a heavy, emphatic "th".

English tip: You already have these sounds in English – use them!

Vowel Length

- (short) vs -- (long).
° (short) vs °° (long).
_ (short) vs __ (long).
Long vowels are held for about twice as long as short ones.

English tip: Compare "bit" (short i) with "beat" (long e) – that's the difference in length.

Shadda & Madda

² (shadda) – double the consonant: inna (إِنَّ) – pronounce both n's.
~ (madda) – prolong the vowel: āyah (آيَة) – stretch the "aa".

English tip: Shadda is like the double "n" in "penknife" – you pronounce both n's.

Separator (¯)

The overline ¯ is a bridge between a prefix and its root. It is not pronounced – it simply marks the boundary, like a silent hyphen.

English tip: Like the hyphen in "re‑write" – it tells you where the parts connect, but you don't pronounce it.
